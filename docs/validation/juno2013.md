# Juno 2012–13: deep-space burns and the Earth flyby

Juno left Earth in August 2011 on a two-year loop: two engine burns in
deep space in August and September 2012 set up a return to Earth, and the
Earth flyby of 9 October 2013 sent it on to Jupiter. These checks fly that
stretch, from 25 August 2012 to 30 November 2013, with the app's kernel
against the navigation team's reconstruction:
- the burns, against NASA's figures;
- every coast between them, about the Sun, where sunlight on Juno's 72 m²
  of solar arrays is the largest force the kernel's gravity leaves out;
- the Earth flyby, flown through, and the "flyby anomaly";
- the app's mission file, flown as the app flies it.

`npm test` runs `src/physics/missions.validate.mjs` (all the missions on
this pattern; `node src/physics/missions.validate.mjs juno2013` for this
one). The numbers below are from November 2026.

## Why Juno

It is a mission none of the others is: two large burns far from any
planet, steered about the Sun, then a gravity assist that changed Juno's
speed about the Sun by 13,800 km/h with no engine at all. It also tests the
kernel close to the Earth at 14.6 km/s, 560 km up, where the Earth's
gravity beyond J2 matters, and it is one of the flybys the flyby anomaly
was looked for in.

## The reference

- **Trajectory:** JPL's Juno navigation reconstruction
  (`spk_rec_110805_*_260824`) through
  [JPL Horizons](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/horizons/), object −61. ICRF
  axes, km and km/s, UTC.
- **Sampling:** `npm run build:missions -- juno2013`
  (`src/physics/missions.build.mjs`, with the mission's facts in
  `src/physics/missions.data.mjs`) fetches Juno every hour about the Sun for
  the whole span, every 10 s through each event it finds, and every minute
  about the Earth for twelve hours around the flyby. Horizons' answers are
  cached in `~/.cache/digital-orrery/missions/juno2013`. It writes the
  fixture `src/physics/fixtures/juno2013.json` (387 kB) and the app's file
  `public/missions/juno2013.json` (547 kB).
- **Near a planet, about the planet.** Close to the Earth the trajectory is
  fetched about the Earth and moved onto the app's Earth. Fetched about the
  Sun, the pass left 0.12 m/s that no force explained: the Earth of
  Horizons' ephemeris and the app's (DE442) are metres apart about the Sun,
  and at a 560 km perigee each metre is a gravity gradient worth about
  7 mm/s over the pass. About the Earth the same pass leaves 1.4 mm/s.

## Finding the burns

The build flies every hour of the span with the kernel's gravity and
compares it with the next hour. An hour whose velocity is more than 5 mm/s
from the kernel's is an event; 10 are found. Each is fetched every 10 s
and flown again step by step, now in the full environment (the Earth's
field to degree 20, sunlight on the arrays), so that the Earth's gravity at
perigee is not taken for thrust. An event of under 0.05 m/s is a join
between navigation solutions; the rest are burns.

- Both deep-space burns are within NASA's rounding: 0.28 and 0.06 m/s off
  figures given to the whole m/s (held to 0.5 m/s plus 0.1 %).
- DSM-1's length is NASA's to the second (held to 2 s plus the 10 s
  sampling); DSM-2's "30 minute firing" is a round figure (held to 30 s).
- DSM-2 ignites at NASA's 3:30 p.m. PDT to the second. NASA's release for
  DSM-1 gives 6:57 p.m. EDT (22:57 UTC), which the trajectory places 27
  minutes after ignition, near cutoff: likely when it was reported, not when
  it lit.
- The four corrections NASA did not publish are found in the trajectory
  and named by date. The one on 1 May 2013 is a single 10 s step, so the
  reconstruction holds it as an impulse.

## The joins

Two small events are steps between navigation solutions: 7.0 mm/s on 12
June 2013, and 1.4 mm/s at the flyby, which is the kernel's own residual
through perigee (below). A third, on 24 August 2012, carried a 5.4 km step
in position between two solutions: the span starts after it, as no flight
could follow it.

Between events, an hour of gravity alone leaves 0.20 mm/s (median; 0.68 at
the 99th percentile): sunlight on the arrays, about 1.2e-7 m/s².

- **Sunlight** is the cannonball model (environment.js): Cr 1.3 on the
  three arrays' 71.6 m² ([2], [4]), with Juno's mass falling at each burn by
  the rocket equation (Isp 318 s, from DSM-1's 376 kg for 344 m/s). One
  strength fitted to all the arcs at once is 91 % of that, and cuts the rms
  at the arcs' ends from 2,062 km (gravity alone) to 139 km.
- **What is left** grows with the arc: under 5 km for the first three
  weeks, hundreds over the seven-month cruise. A sunlit flat plate is not
  Juno: the arrays tilt off the Sun as the high-gain antenna follows the
  Earth, and the body and the heat they radiate push too. One fitted
  strength serves the long arcs better than the short ones, which do better
  as built. The navigation team's model has the attitude; the kernel's has
  a sphere.
- **Checks:** every arc within 500 km with sunlight fitted; the fit at
  least ten times better than gravity alone; the fitted strength within
  15 % of the craft as built.

## The Earth flyby

| | Published | The reconstruction | The kernel through it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Closest approach | 19:21 UTC [5] | 19:21:25 UTC | 19:21:25 UTC |
| Height | 559 km [6] | 561.1 km above the ellipsoid (554.4 above the equatorial radius) at 34.3° S, 33.9° E | within 5 m of it |
| Speed about the Sun | 126,000 to 140,000 km/h [5] | 125,531 to 139,377 km/h | — |
| Change in velocity | "about 7.3 km/s" (16,330 mph) [4] | 7.222 km/s | — |

- **Height:** the sources differ by 3 km (347 to 350 miles; the press kit's
  500 km is the planned figure). Over South Africa the ellipsoid is 6.7 km
  below the equatorial radius, so the height above it is the one to
  compare. Held to 3 km.
- **Through the pass:** the kernel flies from six hours before to six
  after in the full environment. It ends 35 m and 1.8 mm/s from the
  reconstruction. With J2 as the Earth's only zonal term it would end
  0.72 km and 36 mm/s off: the field beyond J2 is a 20-fold improvement
  here.
- **Speed about the Sun:** the Earth's velocity plus the incoming and
  outgoing asymptotes', within 500 km/h of the release's figures given to the
  thousand.
- **Change in velocity:** the asymptotes' difference, 2 v∞ sin(turn/2),
  is 7.222 km/s against the press kit's 7.300. The kit does not say how it
  measured it; held to 0.1 km/s.

### The flyby anomaly

Anderson et al. [7] found six Earth flybys whose speed after the pass
differed from the prediction by up to 13 mm/s, fitted by
Δv∞ = K v∞ (cos δin − cos δout), K = 2ωR/c, with δ the declinations of
the asymptotes. For Juno (v∞ 10.39 km/s, δ 14.2° in and 39.5° out) the
formula gives 6.4 mm/s. The kernel, which has no such term, ends the pass
with a v∞ 0.47 mm/s from the reconstruction's: the reconstruction, fitted
to Juno's tracking through the flyby, shows no anomaly of that size.
Checked: within 1 mm/s.

## In the app

Choose **Juno · Earth flyby, 2012–13** under Real missions, or
`?mission=juno2013`. The craft starts on 25 August 2012 with the press
kit's mass and arrays, and every burn is scripted as flown, steered about
the Sun (and the Earth near it).

`missions.validate.mjs` case 5 flies the file as the app does, through
the Craft model and the kernel host in hour-long frames (a minute within a
day of the flyby):

| | Perigee | At the end | Worst | Added |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Every burn replayed as flown | −14.8 km (hits the Earth) | — | — | — |
| Burns aimed from the craft (the default) | 561.8 km (flown: 561.8) | 0.2 km | 210 km | 0.38 m/s |

- **Blind,** small differences in the deep-space burns and the sunlight
  grow over a year, and the flyby magnifies what arrives: Juno flown blind
  hits the Earth.
- **Aimed,** each burn after DSM-1 is worked out from where this craft is,
  as the navigation team's were, aimed at the start of the next burn, or at
  the flyby's perigee when one comes first (Newton's method doesn't
  converge across a flyby). The aims fly in the craft's own environment
  (`aimInEnvironment` in the file): over seven months sunlight moves Juno
  6,000 km, and aims in gravity alone missed by that much and spent
  930 m/s chasing it. Artemis's aims, over days, stay in gravity alone.
- **Checks:** aimed, within 5 km at the end, 500 km throughout, the
  perigee within 5 km of the flown, for under 5 m/s.
